Six pieces of feedback, and what the brief says about the product today.

The most important problem is that a test marked flaky stays marked flaky, silently, with no cause, no owner, no expiry, and no warning when its behavior changes.

That is how one customer shipped a real regression to production.

Two distinct problems, not one

I tagged each piece of feedback with the job the customer was trying to do, not the feature they asked for.

Flake lifecycle management

Feedback B, C, D, E

Deciding whether a flake matters, fixing it without poisoning its history, knowing when "flaky" stops being true, muting what you can't act on without losing it.

Test segmentation

Feedback A, F

Rerunning only what failed, running subsets, splitting across parallel machines with different configs.

Why lifecycle is the more important problem

I am ranking on severity, not volume. Segmentation costs teams time and money. A stale flaky label costs them a production incident.

It is the only case where Cypress's own mechanism makes things worse rather than merely failing to help.

It is the broadest signal in the sample, with four of six voices.

"A developer merged a PR because the test that was failing had been flagged as flaky before. The next day, that same test failure was a real regression that made it to production."Feedback D

What I am consciously discounting. Feedback F is the most severe single quote and the only explicit churn signal. I am discounting it because it is one voice on a different product surface, and I would weight the prevalence check by revenue, since that one voice may be a large account. That is a judgment call I would want challenged.

One thing: detect when a flaky test's behavior changes, and tell someone

Compare to its baseline

Compare each flaky-flagged test's recent failure pattern to its own baseline.

Trigger

A run of consecutive failures, or a new error message the test has never produced before.

When it fires

The test stops being silently excluded, and the team is notified.

This is the smallest thing that prevents the worst outcome. It is also a prerequisite for anything bigger: quarantine, ownership, and expiry all become safe only once the system can notice a flake turning into a regression.

What I'm leaving open, and how I'd know it worked

Why before muting

Feedback E's request to mute noise is reasonable, but muting without change detection makes D more likely.

Not specified on purpose

The detection threshold, whether state is per-branch, and the notification channel. Each depends on the investigation in the Questions section.

How I'd know it worked

Track alerts a human confirms as a real regression against alerts dismissed as still flaky. A high confirm rate proves the problem exists at scale. A high dismiss rate means the heuristic needs tuning before it earns default-on.

Where I would not trust my own output

The sample

Six curated, anonymized quotes. Complaints self-select. If the data comes back thin, I would downgrade to surfacing retry history and move on.

Severity over frequency

This is a judgment call. If segmentation pain is far more widespread, or is what drives cancellations, I would reverse the ranking and say so.

The frame

I chose trust. A cost frame is equally available from Feedback A and points at rerun-failed-only as the faster return.

The feature's own failure mode

Alerts that fire too often get muted, and we are back to D. The threshold has to be tuned on real data before notifications are on by default.
